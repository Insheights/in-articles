# Where Value Flow Leaks in Your Operating Model

![](images/01.png)

Most strategies make sense in the room where they’re approved. The analysis is sound, the priorities are clear, and everyone around the table agrees. A year later, results fall short. The usual explanation is execution: the teams didn’t deliver, or the wrong people were in the wrong seats.

It’s a convenient explanation, and not only because it’s sometimes true. A strategy is the leadership team’s bets made visible. Admitting a strategy was wrong can feel like admitting their judgment was wrong, and few organizations reward senior leaders for that. So on paper, strategies never fail. Execution does.

Sometimes execution really is the problem. More often, nobody failed. The strategy lost value a little at a time, at every point where work passed from one group to another. No single loss was big enough to notice. Together, they were big enough to force major corrections, usually too late.

In the previous article in this series, I argued that product mindset has to be a capability of the whole company, not a single role. This article looks at what happens when it isn’t: where value leaks, and why effort alone doesn’t stop it.

## One intent, followed all the way

Let’s go back to Stocklight, the illustrative company from the previous article, before it changed how it thinks. Stocklight sells software that gives regional industrial distributors real-time visibility into their warehouse inventory.

Leadership sees renewals slipping and hears one message from distributors: they lose orders when stock runs out. The executive team agrees on a clear intent: help distributors avoid stockouts.

Twelve months later, the forecasting module has shipped on time. Onboarding a new distributor still takes about 90 days. Few inventory managers use the forecasts. Renewals are still slipping. And every team hit its targets.

Here’s where the value went, traced backward from the customer to the boardroom.

## Leak 1: Feedback

Support hears inventory managers asking for alerts rather than forecasts: something that tells them when to act, not something they have to interpret. Sales hears distributors comparing Stocklight with a competitor that now bundles inventory visibility at no extra cost.

The insight exists. It sits in support tickets and call notes. It reaches leadership, if at all, as a quarterly summary averaged into a satisfaction score. So next year’s strategy is built on the same assumptions as this year’s.

It happens at far larger scale, too. A [study of Nokia](https://knowledge.insead.edu/strategy/who-killed-nokia-nokia-did) by Timo Vuori and Quy Huy, based on 76 interviews with the company’s managers and engineers, found that during the smartphone race, middle managers feared how top managers would react to bad news. So they stayed silent or passed along filtered, optimistic information, and top managers formed an overly optimistic view of what the company could deliver. The truth about how far Nokia had fallen behind existed inside the company. It just didn’t travel upward.

**The signal:** ask when your strategy last changed because of something a customer did or said. If nobody can remember, feedback is leaking.

**The missing principle:** continuous discovery. Learning from customers stopped before it reached the people setting strategy.

## Leak 2: Adoption, engagement, and retention

Adoption, engagement, and retention apply to two groups: the customers using what you deliver, and the employees using the new ways of working a strategy depends on. Value leaks on both sides.

Externally, the forecasting module ships to every distributor at once and is marked done. Most inventory managers never start using the forecasts, which sit in a dashboard they don’t open every day. Those who try them rarely act on them, because they don’t trust numbers they don’t understand. With no change in their stockouts, distributors have no new reason to renew, and renewals keep slipping.

Internally, the same pattern plays out inside Stocklight. Sales receives new messaging built around forecasting, but few reps use it. Those who do use it only occasionally, and within a quarter most have gone back to the old pitch. Support is trained on the module, but helping customers use it isn’t part of how support is measured, so it rarely comes up.

Because nobody is measured on adoption, engagement, or retention, inside or outside the company, nobody notices for months. Distributors got access, but they didn’t get value. Employees got a new approach, but it didn’t change how they worked. This is the most expensive leak, because, by this point, all the cost has already been spent.

Quibi, the short-form streaming service, is a well-known external example. It [raised $1.75 billion](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/all/quibi-shutting-down-just-months-after-launching-n1244215) before launching in 2020. A month after launch, the company [reported 3.5 million downloads but only 1.3 million active users](https://www.streamtvinsider.com/video/quibi-has-1-3m-active-users-one-month-after-launch). [Sensor Tower estimated](https://techcrunch.com/2020/07/08/report-says-quibi-lost-92-of-its-earliest-users-after-free-trials-expired/) that only about 8% of its first wave of free-trial users went on to pay, a figure Quibi disputed. It shut down about six months after launch. Getting the app was never the problem. Too few people kept using it.

**The signal:** for your last few releases and your last major internal change, check how many people started using them, how many use them regularly, and how many are still using them three months later.

**The missing principles:** outcome over output, and hypothesis-driven thinking. The module was judged by whether it shipped, and it shipped to everyone at once instead of as a test with an expected result.

## Leak 3: Fragmentation and handoffs

Avoiding stockouts takes several functions. Product builds the forecasting. Sales positions it. Installation connects it to each distributor’s systems. Support helps customers use it. Each function gets its piece, and a measure for that piece: product is measured on on-time release, sales on new bookings, installation on installations completed, and support on ticket resolution time. Nobody is measured on whether stockouts fall.

Once an outcome is split into pieces that must pass from team to team, work stalls. A newly signed distributor waits for installation to schedule a visit, then for its data to be connected, then for support to train its staff. Onboarding takes about 90 days, and most of that time is spent waiting in queues, not being worked on.

Context leaks at every transfer, too. The salesperson knew this distributor bought Stocklight because of a painful seasonal stockout problem. Installation received a work order. Support received a ticket. By the time the distributor goes live, nobody serving them remembers why they bought. Every piece succeeds, and the whole still fails.

Boeing’s 787 Dreamliner shows this leak at enormous scale. Boeing [outsourced much of the design, engineering, and manufacturing](https://www.industryweek.com/supply-chain/supplier-relationships/article/21282352/boeings-organizational-problems-date-back-two-decades) of entire sections of the aircraft to more than 50 partners, who could in turn outsource to their own suppliers. Without close coordination between the pieces, integration problems piled up, and the program ended up about three years late and billions of dollars over budget.

**The signal:** list the measures of every function that contributes to an outcome. If none of them is the outcome itself, it has been fragmented. Then compare the total time from start to finish with the time someone was actively working on it. A large gap means value is leaking in the handoffs.

**The missing principle:** cross-functional ownership. Every function owned a piece of the outcome, and no team owned the whole.

## Leak 4: Translation

When the strategy is turned into work, “help distributors avoid stockouts” becomes “build a forecasting module.” The deliverable is misaligned with the intent: inventory managers needed something that told them when to act, not a forecast they had to interpret. It’s also incomplete. The scope covers building the module, but not connecting it to how distributors work, preparing their staff to use it, or checking whether stockouts fall. The project gets approved, and the intent stays behind in the slide deck.

Wells Fargo’s cross-selling strategy is one of the best-known examples. The stated intent was to deepen customer relationships. It was translated into a target of eight products per customer, known inside the bank as “Eight is great,” and backed by aggressive sales goals. Under that pressure, employees opened [millions of accounts customers hadn’t authorized](https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2016/09/20/494680201/wells-fargo-ceo-to-address-accounts-scandal-before-senate-panel), and in 2016 the bank paid $185 million in fines. The target survived the translation. The intent didn’t.

**The signal:** ask a team working on a strategic initiative what customer outcome it’s meant to change. If they describe the deliverable instead, the intent has leaked. Then check whether the scope includes everything the outcome needs, or ends at delivery.

**The missing principle:** customer obsession. The work started from what the company could build, not from what distributors needed to achieve.

## Why good intentions can’t plug the leaks

Notice that every leak happens between teams, not within them. Every team at Stocklight did its job well. The leaks live in the spaces between functions, and nobody owns those spaces.

That’s why the usual fixes disappoint. More alignment meetings share information, but still leave nobody owning the outcome. Better goal-setting helps only if goals cross functions. When each function writes its own, fragmentation just becomes official. A heroic product manager can chase the outcome across every boundary for a while, but that’s the role trap from the previous article, and it burns people out. A new tool can speed up handoffs, but it doesn’t remove them or decide who owns the result.

Each fix adds effort. None changes ownership. That’s because people don’t cause the leaks. The operating model causes them: how the organization turns intent into work, divides that work, measures it, and moves information through it. Look back at the four leaks, and each one traces to part of it. Feedback leaks through how information moves. Adoption, engagement, and retention leak through what gets measured. Fragmentation and handoffs come from how work is divided. Translation happens in how intent is turned into work. An operating model organized around functions works well inside each function. The leaks appear in the spaces between them, which it was never designed to manage.

The operating model creates the leaks. Fear of failure keeps them hidden, because the leaks are almost always diagnosed as execution failures. When a strategy can’t be seen to fail, every miss gets traced to the teams below it, and the fix is more pressure, new people, or another reorganization. The spaces between teams, where the value actually leaked, are never examined because doing so would mean asking whether the plan itself was sound.

## Replacing fear of failure

Leaders who openly admit a strategy was wrong are rare. One recent example is Klarna. After replacing much of its customer service with AI, its CEO [acknowledged in 2025](https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/klarna-ceo-reverses-course-by-hiring-more-humans-not-ai/491396) that the push had focused too much on cost and led to lower-quality service, and the company began hiring human agents again. What made the admission credible wasn’t the words. It was that the company changed what it did.

That points to a simple test for a genuine admission of strategic failure. It names the strategy, not the execution or how things looked. It comes from the person who owns the strategy. It comes early enough to matter. And it’s followed by a change in what the organization does. Few admissions pass all four.

The operating model is where the leaks come from, but a new operating model won’t hold if the organization still treats a missed strategy as a personal failure. Fear of failure isn’t a character flaw. It’s what organizations produce when they reward confident plans and punish visible reversals. Replacing it takes four shifts in how leaders treat their own strategies.

**Accept experiments.** A strategy is a bet, whether it’s written that way or not. Leaders who say so openly make it safe for everyone else to test rather than defend. At Stocklight, the forecasting module could have gone to a handful of distributors first, with a stated expectation that their stockouts would fall.

**Be open to feedback.** Bad news loses detail every time it moves up a level. Leaders have to make it safe to deliver, and go looking for it rather than waiting for it. Stocklight’s support team already knew inventory managers wanted alerts, not forecasts. That insight needed a direct path to the people setting the strategy, not a quarterly average.

**Pivot with data.** When evidence shows the plan is wrong, change course without waiting for next year’s planning cycle. Changing direction based on evidence isn’t inconsistency. It’s the point of collecting evidence at all. Once Stocklight could see that distributors needed alerts, shifting the roadmap should have been a routine decision, not a political one.

**Be ready to reject.** The hardest shift is stopping something you championed. Name in advance the signals that would end an initiative, and treat ending it as a good decision made early, not a failure admitted late. If a small pilot had shown that inventory managers weren’t using the forecasts, stopping before a full rollout would have saved most of the cost.

Fear of failure doesn’t stop a strategy from being wrong. It only delays finding out until being wrong is expensive.

## Find your own leaks

Pick one initiative that mattered to your organization in the past year, and trace it backward from the customer to the boardroom. At each point where the work changed hands, ask three questions:

Did the receiving team know what outcome the work was for?

How long did the work wait before anyone picked it up?

Who would have noticed if the outcome failed?

Then ask one final question: what does the customer do differently now?

You’ll likely find the leaks. Not inside any one team’s work, but between them.
