# LinkedIn post: Let Customer Behavior Write Your Next Hypothesis

The companion post for [this article](article.md), as published.

---

Your customers will tell you what they want next. Their behavior will tell you something else.

When the two disagree, behavior is usually closer to the job, and words closer to the explanation.

Asked what they wanted next, the inventory managers at Stocklight, the illustrative company in my value creation series, said: better suggested quantities.

Their orders said something else. The logs showed managers adding items that hadn't alerted, by hand. No one had mentioned it at a single discovery visit. Shown their own orders, three managers gave the same answer: their suppliers waive freight above an order minimum, so they top the order up to clear it.

The behavior showed the problem. The words explained it.

A signal isn't a hypothesis yet. A hypothesis has five parts:
1. What we saw: a behavior, in whose actions, how often.
2. What we believe causes it, stated so it could be false.
3. The smallest change that would test it.
4. What we expect: a leading indicator, a target, a date.
5. What would prove us wrong, decided before the test starts.

That raises the question a reader asked on the last article: where does the first target come from, when there are no readings yet?

Two steps:\
→ Work back from the outcome to a floor. In every 100 orders, 55 started below the minimum and 45 shipped below it. A third fewer was a saving distributors would notice, and that meant about 45% of below-minimum orders clearing it. The floor also had to sit well above the 18% managers already cleared by hand.\
→ Decide in advance what each reading means. Below 45%, reject. From 45% to 65%, keep and retune. Above 65%, widen.

At six weeks: 52%, and orders shipping below the minimum fell from 45 in 100 to 26. Inside the band, so the team kept it and retuned.

A first band isn't a forecast. It's the smallest result worth having, with each reading's consequence agreed before anyone sees the number.

The article includes a template for a behavior-led hypothesis: what you saw, what you'll test, and the band that decides it.
