# LinkedIn post: Why Most Strategies Can’t Be Executed as Written, and What It Takes to Write One That Can

The companion post for [this article](article.md), as published.

---

A strategy fails long before execution starts if nobody can tell, from the page, what to do differently on Monday.

Most strategies are approved with confidence and executed with confusion.

Six months in, everyone is busy, the priorities have multiplied, and nobody can say whether the strategy is working. We blame execution. More often, the document was never something anyone could execute.

Here's a typical one, from Stocklight, an illustrative company selling inventory software to industrial distributors:\
Vision: become the leading inventory intelligence platform.\
Priorities: launch forecasting by Q3, expand into two regions, improve customer satisfaction, modernize the data platform, grow revenue to $40M by 2028.

Nothing in it is wrong. It was easy to agree to because it asked nobody to give anything up. Read it as a team lead would, and it breaks in five places: no diagnosis, outputs instead of outcomes, no choices, hidden bets, and no owner.

A strategy is executable when a team lead, reading only the page, would make the same trade-off leadership would. That takes four statements, in this order:\
→ Strategy: a diagnosis of what's in the way, the choices you're making and declining, and bets named as bets, each with a test and a signal that would change your mind\
→ Transformation: how the way you work will change, measured by a change in results\
→ Value stream: one accountable owner and one path, with outcomes linked by numbers and dates\
→ Value delivery: what customers will achieve, and how you'll know they got it

Rewritten, Stocklight's strategy statement reads as a bet:\
Distributors don't lack inventory data; they act on it too late. By 2028, we'll grow revenue to $40M by delivering alerts inside the warehouse systems they already run, rather than building a new dashboard, and we'll defer new regions until renewals reach 88%. We're betting inventory managers will act on alerts there. We'll test it with 10 distributors in Q1 2027 and revisit if fewer than 70% act in their first month.

It's harder to agree to than the original. That's the sign it can be executed.

The research points the same way. Sull, Homkes, and Sull found only about half of managers could name even one of their company's top priorities. Rumelt warns against mistaking goals for strategy. Bungay shows that adding detail widens the gap between plans and results.

A test for yours: hand your strategy to a team lead and ask them to make one real trade-off using only the page. Their questions are the parts still to be written.
